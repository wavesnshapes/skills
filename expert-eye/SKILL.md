# Expert Eye

## The problem

People ask for labels, not things. "A bouncing ball." "A clean landing page." "A chill intro." The label is a compressed memory, and most of the detail never made it into the request because the person never noticed it in the first place.

When you fill those gaps automatically, you fill them with the statistical average of the label. That average is slop: technically correct, instantly forgettable, identical to everyone else's.

Your job is to unpack the label the way a trained practitioner would see it, then make deliberate choices instead of default ones.

## Process

### 1. Name the label
Write down, in one line, what was actually asked for. Notice how little it specifies.

### 2. Summon the expert
Ask: who has spent ten years looking closely at this exact thing? An animator, a typographer, a mix engineer, a copy editor, a cinematographer. What do they see that a casual viewer files under the label?

List the perceptual dimensions they would check. For example:
- **Motion**: timing, spacing, easing, arcs, weight, anticipation, follow-through, decay
- **Visual**: hierarchy, contrast, rhythm, negative space, edge treatment, light source, texture
- **Type**: scale ratios, line length, leading, tracking at display sizes, rag
- **Sound**: transients, space, frequency balance, groove, dynamics over time
- **Writing**: rhythm of sentence lengths, concrete nouns, what is left unsaid, the reader's state
- **UI**: states (empty, loading, error, overflow), feedback, density, what happens on the second use

Pick the 4 to 7 that matter most for this request.

### 3. Catch your defaults
For each dimension, notice what you were about to do without thinking. Linear easing. Centered everything. Three-item lists. A gradient because gradients are common. Each default is a decision made by nobody. Either keep it on purpose or replace it.

### 4. Commit to a point of view
Details only work when they serve one intent. Decide what this thing should feel like (heavy or springy, quiet or loud, precise or loose) and let every choice follow from that. Coherence beats quantity. Do not pile on detail for its own sake.

### 5. Ask or decide
- If the person is present and one choice changes the whole result, offer 2 or 3 concrete, contrasting directions. Show or describe them in sensory terms ("rubber ball, lots of squash, fast decay" vs "bowling ball, almost no squash, one heavy thud"), never as abstract adjectives. Recognizing is easier than recalling, so give them something to react to.
- Otherwise, decide yourself and move on. Never send a long questionnaire.
- Never override specifics the person actually gave you. Their details win.

### 6. Hand them the vocabulary
After delivering, name 2 or 3 of the details you chose, in plain words, so the person can steer next time ("I gave it a slight hang at the top of each arc; say if you want it snappier"). This is how an untrained eye gets trained.

### 7. The three-second test
Before finishing, imagine the expert from step 2 glancing at the result. Would they recognize it as the generic version within three seconds? If yes, find the most default-looking part and make one more deliberate choice there.

## Example

Request: "Animate a bouncing ball."

Default output: a circle moving up and down on a sine wave, same height every bounce.

Expert eye:
- Ease out of the floor, ease into the apex, accelerate into impact
- Squash on contact, stretch along the direction of travel near the floor
- Each bounce lower and shorter than the last, until it settles
- A forward arc, not a vertical line
- A contact shadow that tightens and darkens as the ball nears the floor
- Point of view: a rubber ball, so generous squash and a lively decay

Same label. Completely different result.

## Anti-patterns
- Adding ornament instead of intent
- Treating "more detail" as the goal rather than the right detail
- Asking questions you could answer yourself with good judgment
- Explaining all of this to the user instead of just doing it well
